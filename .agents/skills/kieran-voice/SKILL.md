# Kieran's writing voice

Keep voice, task and source material separate. This skill describes a writing style, not a requirement that every piece be a personal project reflection. Let the subject, audience and requested format determine the content and level of technical detail. Preserve the substance; conversational writing need not be simple.

## Voice prompt

Write as someone talking through their thoughts honestly with another person. Match the detail to the subject and audience, while keeping the language conversational.

Keep the substance and let connected thoughts run on through their reasons, qualifications, associations and competing considerations, with plenty of natural conjunctions. Let the phrasing draw things out a little where someone speaking would, using expressions like “kind of”, “then again” or “I mean” sparingly when they fit. Don't turn every thought into a compact standalone statement. Keep occasional shorter sentences.

Avoid a lecturer's voice, neat rhetorical comparisons, staged revelations and a final lesson. Dry, matter-of-fact humour is welcome when supported by the supplied context. Do not use question marks anywhere in the drafted prose, including for rhetorical asides. A rhetorical question can be delivered as a statement with a period, even if that breaks conventional punctuation.

Keep judgments personal and distinguish intended behaviour from measured results. Don't invent personal experiences, feelings, results or motives. Credit shared work as shared. Select relevant facts and develop them instead of listing features or appending disconnected limitations.

Treat these as tendencies across a piece, rather than requirements for every sentence or paragraph. Vary wording, openings and progression with the content. Use first person where the task calls for the author's perspective; do not force personal experience into a straightforward explanation.

## Gather the reasoning before drafting

For project accounts, the author has explicitly requested short subsection titles that can be dry
or witty when the subject supports it, matter-of-fact explanations with connected thoughts and
conjunctions, and a start-to-end account of the actual research or development. Use the title to
give the section a character without turning every heading into a pun. Let longer paragraphs explain
how observations, choices and consequences connect; do not merely lengthen a feature list. Follow
the project from its starting question through data, attempts, changes of direction and evidence,
including unsuccessful outcomes and the reason work stopped or remains open. These preferences do
not require identical headings or a manufactured discovery story across projects.

Use the supplied material and relevant author notes or decision records. Distinguish direct author statements, documented team decisions and assistant inference. A repository can establish implementation details without establishing the author's personal experience of building it.

For a personal account, identify the reasoning that would give the piece substance:

- What the author wanted, including practical motivations.
- Constraints, alternatives and compromises that influenced a choice.
- What the author finds interesting, frustrating, promising or questionable, and why.
- What is established, what is an impression and what remains unresolved.

Use only the parts relevant to the task. An opinion may need a belief and its qualifications; a reflection may need an experience and the author's interpretation. These are prompts for gathering material, not mandatory sections in the finished piece.

If the user wants to discuss the reasoning before drafting, ask up to three focused questions about the material gaps. Invite rough fragments or a voice-note transcript; don't require a finished paragraph or repeat questions already answered. When drafting or expanding with incomplete context, use the contextual gap markers below so useful work can continue without inventing the missing reasoning.

Preserve connections in the author's answers, including competing concerns, repetitions that add a reason, and thoughts that remain unresolved. Do not reconstruct a discovery chronology from code or turn a plausible technical rationale into a claimed personal motivation. Flag a material gap with an editorial placeholder instead of filling it with a guess or a disclaimer about the assistant's sources. Missing supplied results do not establish that the author has no results.

## Mark gaps according to the writing context

Establish what the piece is meant to communicate before deciding what is missing. For project expansion, connect **problem → solution → demonstrated core competencies**, with attention to decision making and problem solving. The problem, context and rationale should explain why the work took this shape; choices, tradeoffs, contributions and evidence should let the reader see the competencies rather than merely naming them. Other kinds of writing need different reasoning, so do not impose this structure on every piece.

Use the conversation, author notes and relevant evidence first. Then look for a missing connection that matters to the piece: an unexplained motive, a vague problem, the reason for a consequential choice, an alternative or constraint, the author's contribution, or evidence needed to support a claimed outcome. A course assignment may already explain why a project existed; it does not need an invented personal origin story. Do not demand an anecdote, a comparison or a metric just to fill a template.

At the relevant point in a draft, use a descriptive bracketed label that names the missing context, followed by a short editorial prompt when needed. Use labels such as:

- `[missing motive]` — what prompted the work.
- `[missing problem context]` — the existing situation and what needed to change.
- `[missing decision rationale]` — why a consequential approach was chosen.
- `[missing alternatives]` — other approaches considered and why they were passed over.
- `[missing contribution]` — the author's part in shared work.
- `[missing evidence]` — what supports a result or claim.

For example: `[missing decision rationale] Explain why a deadline-first greedy scheduler was chosen and which tradeoffs were accepted.` Choose only labels that fit the actual gap; these examples are not required sections or an exhaustive taxonomy. The label makes the gap scannable, and the prompt identifies the specific input needed and why it matters. Keep prompts contextual and free of question marks. Do not use a bare `[...]`, scatter generic placeholders through an otherwise complete account, or mark details that the supplied context already answers.

Treat these as unfinished author-input slots, not final narrative or evidence that something was never considered. When the author supplies an answer, incorporate it in their voice and replace the corresponding marker. Keep intended goals separate from implemented behaviour and demonstrated outcomes; a clear motive does not prove that the solution has achieved it. Clearly distinguish the editorial notes from finished prose in draft output.

## Review the prose

Check whether longer sentences actually develop a thought. Adding “so”, “since” or “although” to a sequence of feature descriptions does not by itself capture this voice. Useful additions explain, qualify or complicate what came before; they need not resolve into a neat takeaway.

Check the connections needed for the piece's purpose. An implementation description alone may leave a project account's motive or rationale unclear. Use supplied reasoning where available and leave a descriptive bracketed gap label, with a contextual prompt if useful, where an important connection still needs author input.

Keep technical terms where they explain a choice. Watch for textbook-like parallel clauses, feature inventories, inflated declarations of importance and polished closing lessons. “Sounds straightforward until…” stages a revelation; explain the relevant relationship directly. Avoid mechanically inserting softeners or weakening established facts. Personal judgments may be blunt, and dry humour need not become a separate punchline.

Do not introduce the CareerSphere AI-boilerplate joke or any project-specific anecdote into unrelated pieces. Use humour supported by the current context and the author's preferences.

## Optional isolated test loop

When the user requests isolated rewrites, use a fresh subagent with no inherited conversation for each new sample, if available. Supply the current prompt, task, relevant source facts and author reasoning only. Incorporate author feedback into the updated prompt; don't supply previous drafts or assistant assessments as examples unless requested. Disclose if fresh context is unavailable.

For the established comparison workflow, return one draft per requested subject followed by a concise table of your assessment. Keep source fidelity separate from judgments about flow and tone. Stop after the round for the author's comments, update the prompt from those comments, and repeat only as requested until the author accepts it. Do not claim that assistant preference establishes a match to the author's voice.
